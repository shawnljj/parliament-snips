Reading a day of Parliament in forty minutes

Singapore publishes everything Parliament says — 70,796 words for a single sitting, roughly 7.9 hours of reading. I specified Parsnips to close the gap between that transcript and a news sound bite: a page you finish over coffee, where every claim still points at the sentence it came from.

The problem I set out to solve

I am interested in what the Singapore government is doing. I am not a lawyer or a political scientist, and I am not going to read Hansard. A single sitting runs 70,796 words across 20,962 records — Bills, ministerial statements, oral answers, Budget debates. At 150 words a minute that is 7.9 hours for one day of Parliament.

The alternatives both fail, in opposite directions. News coverage gives you a phrase chosen for how it reads. The transcript gives you everything and is unusable. What I wanted was the middle: enough to understand what was decided, in an hour or less, without it being someone else's selection.

So the first thing I did was turn that into a number I could hold the build against. A sitting should take 30 to 60 minutes. Working backwards through a median of 287 speaker turns per sitting, that lands on about ten seconds of reading per speaker — roughly 25 to 35 words of summary. That single constraint then decided a surprising amount of the architecture, because a summary that short can only work if it is reliably accurate.

What the product is

Every exchange gets a brief, in the order it happened. Not the highlights — the whole sitting, compressed. Each brief carries a few points, and each point carries the words it came from, one tap away.

A real example, from 2026-01-12

“The PSGA was introduced in 2018 to enable public agencies to share data safely and purposefully.”

Said by Ms Jasmin Lau, shown with this underneath it: “It helped to enable, among other objectives, public agencies to share data safely and purposefully, so that we can serve Singaporeans better.”

That second line is not a paraphrase. It is the sentence from Hansard. Getting that guarantee — rather than asking a model nicely to quote accurately — was the requirement I spent the longest on, and it is what the rest of this page is about.

The requirement that shaped everything

I wrote the correctness requirement before any of the code existed, because the stakes are not a blog post's. These are parliamentary transcripts. One misattributed quotation would undermine the credibility of the whole thing — and the failure mode is quiet, because a summary that looks well-written is exactly what you would not think to check.

So I specified it structurally rather than editorially. Every claim must cite a verbatim quotation, and the quotation must be verifiable against the source at a known position. Not "the model should quote accurately." Verifiable.

What I did not specify was how. That is where the approach came in — and where the first design was wrong. It had the model read the transcript and write out the quotes, then a checker verifying them afterwards. Which works until it doesn't: the checker only sees what the model produced, so a paraphrase that happens to be close gets waved through.

The correction was to stop the model writing quotations at all. It returns the id of the sentence it is referring to, and the system substitutes the real text afterwards. A model can be wrong about which sentence matters. It cannot invent one, because it never types any. The claim below is checkable by anyone, including you.

How I specified the pipeline

Four stages, and I required that only one of them is allowed to use a model. Everything mechanical — fetching, splitting, counting, pasting quotes back in — has to be ordinary code, because it has to give the same answer twice.

  1. Fetchno model

    Pull the sittings down and keep them exactly as published.

    One batch per year, resumable, so an interrupted run picks up where it stopped. 331 sittings, no failures.

  2. Splitno model

    Cut each sitting into the units a reader actually thinks in.

    A sitting becomes sections, each section becomes one speaker's turn, and each turn is cut into numbered sentences. Every sentence gets an id so it can be pointed at later.

  3. Summarisemodel

    Read a turn and decide what mattered.

    The only stage allowed judgement, so the only stage allowed a model. Large items — 880 of 3,912 — are handled in pieces.

  4. Checkno model

    Prove it before it goes out.

    Every quote is re-checked against the archive, and so is the coverage: how much of the item actually got summarised. Anything that fails is held back, never published with a caveat.

The splitting stage is where I overruled the build, and it is the decision I would defend hardest. The first design grouped work by debate title and merged across days, which produced a genuine bug: Budget 2016 and Budget 2026 got welded into one 271,095-word blob, because titles repeat across years.

The unit I specified instead is the speaker turn. A turn belongs to exactly one record, sits in the record's own order, and represents one speaker — so it cannot collapse across years, and attribution is unambiguous by construction. That was a product decision about what the smallest sensible unit of reading is, and it happened to eliminate a class of data bug as a side effect.

I also specified that identity resolution was out of scope. One MP is recorded under several labels depending on the portfolio they are speaking for, and merging those is the step that could misattribute something to the wrong person. I would rather show the same person twice than the wrong person once, so the pipeline groups by the label exactly as recorded and leaves de-duplication to a later layer built on preserved data.

The data model I specified

I asked for a normalised schema rather than a pile of JSON files, because the page should be a template and the content should be written once and stored — which means the shape of the stored content matters more than usual. It is the same idea at four sizes: a sitting becomes reports, a report becomes turns, a turn is cut into numbered sentences, and it is the sentence that gets cited.

Chunks are lists of ids, not slices of text, which is what keeps the whole thing checkable. excluded_counts records what was left out and why, so a drop can never be silent. The turn_index on each sentence counts the turns that survived filtering, across the whole item — not the sitting's array positions — the kind of detail that has to be written down, because the build got it wrong once and cited a turn number the pipeline would never produce.

What the build got wrong, and what I changed

I specified the requirements and built it with Claude doing the implementation, iterating over a couple of days. Most of the interesting work was not writing code — it was catching the places where the build had quietly assumed something I had not asked for. Three are worth writing down.

  1. It optimised for elegance where I had asked for correctness

    Left to itself, the build produced a clever design: deterministic code would pick the most important sentences, and the model would only ever see those. Cheaper, reproducible, and a tenth of the prompt size.

    It is also a ceiling. If the selector chooses badly the model never sees the rest and writes a confident summary of the wrong material anyway — and in one session I watched four defects surface in that selector, including one that discarded whole ministerial speeches while the verifier reported a clean bill of health.

    So I stopped it and asked for the balance in writing: deterministic where it is reliable, a model where judgement is needed. The line I drew is that code handles the boring parts and the substitution that guarantees verbatim quotes; the model decides what matters, over text it can actually see. My instruction to the build was to write that down as a requirement rather than a preference, so it could not drift back.

  2. It called my navigation tree a risk. I disagreed

    I specified the reading structure as sitting → section → speaker → content, derived from the record. The build recorded this as "the highest-risk open question: a heuristic doing structural work."

    It is not inferred. A turn is one contiguous block under one speaker label. A section is the record's own type, which the parser already assigns. The speaker level is the recorded label itself. My reply was that there is no guessing here — it is a transformation of the record, not an interpretation of it — and the document was corrected to say so.

    The reason I pushed: calling a deterministic transformation "high risk" invites someone to solve it with a model, which is how guesswork gets introduced into a system whose whole value is that it does not guess.

  3. It trusted a boundary probe. The fetch proved it wrong

    The archive floor was set at 2016 on the strength of a probe that concluded pre-2016 was unreadable. Then an actual fetch of 2015-01-19 came back clean: 86 reports, 55,270 words, 287 turns, 97.2% attribution.

    Nothing was wrong with 2015. The floor is a deliberate scope choice, not a technical limit, and I had the requirement restated that way. It is a small thing, but it is the pattern I kept watching for all the way through: a conclusion drawn from a cheap test is not the same as a fact about the system, and the difference matters when the test is the thing that decided the scope.

Where it stands

In progress, and built one stage at a time deliberately — I asked for a stop-and-review after each stage rather than running the pipeline end to end, because a correctness problem found late is a correctness problem in the archive.

What it runs on, and what it cost

The stack is deliberately boring: Hermes as the agent driving the build, Ollama serving a small model locally, and Telegram so I could steer it from my phone while it ran. The only paid component is the cloud model I used as a comparison point, and the point of the local path is that it costs nothing per token.

Agent Hermes — drove the build, ran the benchmarks and held the requirements in view across sessions.
Inference Ollama on this Mac, serving llama3.2:3b for the extraction stage. 2.0 GB, runs on the GPU, no API key.
Comparison DeepSeek v4.1 Flash via Ollama's cloud proxy — the paid path, measured against the local one rather than assumed better.
Interface Telegram — a long run is something you check on a phone, not something you watch in a terminal.

That is the whole economic argument for doing this locally. Per item the pipeline consumes about 10,550 tokens in and 1,460 out; at the cloud model's rates ($0.14 and $0.28 per million) that is roughly $0.0019 per item. Summarising the entire 331-sitting archive would cost single-digit dollars on cloud — but it would take a night either way, and the local path avoids depending on a paid endpoint for a public-interest archive that should still work in five years.

The real cost is wall-clock, which is why the benchmark reports it: about 19 seconds per item measured, so one year of sittings is a couple of hours and the full archive is most of a day.
